# Slide 4: Opening Provocation

**The Ricardo Pivot (1821)**

- **1817:** David Ricardo argues machinery benefits everyone.
- **1821:** After seeing wages collapse, he changes his mind: "Machinery may be detrimental to the interests of the class of laborers."
- **Today:** We have the benefit of hindsight. We know technology creates wealth, but policy determines distribution.

**Key Takeaway:** Technology is inevitable. Inclusive growth is a choice.

# Slide 12: Historical Lesson 1

**Engels' Pause (Industrial Revolution)**

- **The Data:** Between 1780 and 1840, productivity rose 46%, but wages rose only 12%.
- **The Gap:** It took **60 years** for workers to feel the benefits.
- **Why?** Institutions (Unions, Education, Safety Nets) lagged behind technology.
- **Our Goal:** Shorten the pause.

---

# Slide 13: Historical Lesson 2

**The Solow Paradox (IT Era)**

- *“You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics.”* (Robert Solow, 1987)
- **The Lesson:** Electrifying a steam factory didn't help until the **workflow** was redesigned.
- **Application:** Paving cow paths (digitizing bad processes) yields zero gain. We must **reimagine** public services, not just add AI to them.

---

# Slide 14: Understanding Technological Revolutions

**The Perez Framework: Five Great Surges**

- **The Pattern:** Since the 1770s, we have seen five technological revolutions:
    1. Industrial Revolution (1771)
    2. Age of Steam & Railways (1829)
    3. Age of Steel & Electricity (1875)
    4. Age of Oil, Automobiles & Mass Production (1908)
    5. Age of Information & Telecommunications (1971)
- **The Claim:** Each follows a remarkably similar pattern of diffusion, crisis, and eventual "Golden Age."
- **AI (2022?):** We may be witnessing the sixth.

---

# Slide 15: The Four Phases of a Revolution

**Installation → Turning Point → Deployment**

1. **Irruption (0-10 years):** New technology emerges. Early adopters experiment. Financial capital floods in.
2. **Frenzy (10-20 years):** Speculation explodes. Infrastructure is overbuilt. Paper wealth decouples from real economy. **We are likely here.**
3. **Turning Point:** Bubble bursts. Crisis. Regulatory reckoning. Institutional reconfiguration begins.
4. **Synergy/Deployment (20-40 years):** Technology spreads broadly. Productivity gains are shared. "Golden Ages" emerge (Victorian boom, Post-war prosperity).

---

# Slide 16: Why Bubbles Are a Feature, Not a Bug

**The Mobilization of Capital**

- **The Paradox:** Bubbles are destructive, yet essential.
- **The Function:** Irrational exuberance mobilizes capital at a scale that rational calculation never would.
    - Railways were wildly overbuilt in the 1840s → but we got the infrastructure.
    - Fiber optic cables were massively over-deployed in the 1990s → but we got cheap bandwidth.
    - AI data centers are being built at unprecedented scale now → ...
- **The Lesson:** The question is not "will there be a crash?" but "what infrastructure will remain when the music stops?"

---

# Slide 17: The Turning Point - What Happens Next?

**From Installation to Deployment**

- **The Crisis:** Financial crashes (1847, 1929, 2000) mark the end of the Installation phase.
- **The Reckoning:** Society demands regulation. The excesses of "Gilded Ages" give way to reform.
- **The Opportunity:** This is when policy matters most. The institutional choices made in the Turning Point determine whether the next 30 years are a "Golden Age" or continued instability.
- **For AI:** We have a narrow window to shape the institutional framework before deployment locks in.

---

# Slide 18: Critical Challenge 1 - Financial Stability

**Systemic Risk & Procyclicality**

- **Herding:** If all banks/agencies use the same foundation models for risk assessment, they will identify the same risks and react identically at the same time.
- **The Result:** Massive, synchronized market movements (Procyclicality).
- **The Perez Warning:** In the Frenzy phase, financial capital dominates. It seeks returns, not deployment. We must prepare for the correction.
- **Policy Action:** Regulators must demand "Model Diversity" and stress-test for algorithmic correlation, not just individual institutional risk.

---

# Slide 19: Critical Challenge 2 - Labor Markets (Overview)

**Jobs, Tasks, and the Dual Effect**

- **The Dual Effect of Technology:**
    - **Displacement Effect:** Automation replaces labor in existing tasks.
    - **Reinstatement Effect:** New technology creates entirely new tasks and jobs.
- **The Historical Record:** In every technological revolution, both effects occur. The question is timing and distribution.
- **The AI Difference:** For the first time, automation targets *cognitive* tasks at scale—analysis, writing, coding, diagnosis.

---

# Slide 20: Thinking in Tasks, Not Jobs

**The Task-Based Framework**

- **The Insight (Acemoglu & Restrepo):** Jobs are bundles of tasks. Technology doesn't eliminate jobs wholesale—it automates specific tasks within jobs.
- **Example - Policy Analyst:**
    - Tasks automated: Literature review, first drafts, data summarization
    - Tasks augmented: Strategic synthesis, stakeholder judgment, political navigation
    - Tasks created: Prompt engineering, AI oversight, algorithmic accountability
- **The Implication:** The job persists, but its composition changes. The *value* shifts from execution to judgment.

---

# Slide 21: The Displacement Concern

**Who Is Most Exposed?**

- **Routine Cognitive Work:** Tasks that are rule-based, predictable, and information-intensive are most exposed.
    - Back-office processing, basic legal research, financial analysis, customer service, coding
- **The Skill Paradox:** Unlike previous automation waves, AI affects the *middle* and *upper-middle* of the skill distribution—precisely the jobs that absorbed displaced manufacturing workers.
- **Geographic & Demographic Concentration:** Displacement will not be evenly distributed. Some regions, sectors, and demographics will bear disproportionate costs.

---

# Slide 22: The Compensating Effects

**Why This Time Might Also Be Different (Positively)**

- **Productivity Complementarity:** Many studies show AI augments rather than replaces—30-50% productivity gains for knowledge workers who adopt.
- **Cost Reduction → Demand Expansion:** As cognitive services become cheaper, demand expands (more legal access, more personalized education, more healthcare).
- **New Task Creation:** Every technological revolution has created tasks we could not imagine beforehand. The question is whether we can accelerate their emergence.
- **The Policy Lever:** Reinstatement is not automatic. It requires investment in skills, institutions, and the conditions for new task creation.

---

# Slide 23: The Experience Trap

**Cutting the Bottom Rungs**

- **The Mechanism:** AI targets routine cognitive tasks—the very tasks juniors use to build expertise.
- **The Problem:** If AI writes the first draft, does the basic analysis, and handles routine queries—how do juniors develop tacit knowledge?
- **The Risk:** We may see high productivity today but a *crisis of expertise* in 5-10 years. The pipeline of senior professionals dries up.
- **Examples:** Junior lawyers, analysts, doctors, accountants, software engineers.

---

# Slide 24: Skills and Expertise in the AI Era

**What Becomes Valuable?**

- **Tacit Knowledge:** Judgment, intuition, and pattern recognition that cannot be easily codified.
- **Visible Expertise:** The ability to demonstrate *why* a decision was made—ethics, context, stakeholder understanding.
- **Orchestration Skills:** Managing AI systems, knowing when to trust output, combining multiple tools effectively.
- **Learning Velocity:** The ability to unlearn and relearn as capabilities shift monthly.

---

# Slide 25: Rethinking Professional Development

**From "Mastering Basics" to "Guided Complexity"**

- **The Old Model:** Learn fundamentals → Practice routine tasks → Build to complexity over years.
- **The New Model:** Engage with complexity early, with AI handling routine execution. Juniors must learn to *evaluate* and *direct*, not just execute.
- **Institutional Response Required:**
    - Restructure apprenticeship and on-the-job training
    - Create "AI-assisted learning" pathways that preserve skill development
    - Measure outcomes (judgment, quality) not just throughput
- **The Goal:** Ensure the experience escalator has rungs, even if they look different.

---

# Slide 26: Critical Challenge 3 - Explainability & Trust (1/2)

**The Black Box Dilemma**

- **The Conflict:** Policy requires accountability and reason. Modern AI (Neural Networks) operates on probability and pattern matching, not logic.
- **The "Explainability Trilemma":** You can usually only have two: Accuracy, Complexity, Explainability. High-performance models are often the least explainable.
- **The Risk:** "Computer says no" is not an acceptable answer for a citizen denied a benefit or a bank denied a license.

---

# Slide 27: Critical Challenge 3 - Explainability & Trust (2/2)

**Operationalizing Trust**

- **From "No" to "Guardrails":** We cannot wait for perfect explainability.
- **Solutions:**
    - **Sandboxes:** Safe spaces with error budgets for experimentation.
    - **"AI Verify":** Testing frameworks (like Singapore's) that benchmark model behavior against safety standards.
    - **Human-Over-The-Loop:** Critical decisions must always have a human accountable for the *outcome*, even if they didn't generate the *output*.

---

# Slide 28: Critical Challenge 4 - The Adoption Gap (1/2)

**The 95% Pilot Trap**

- **The Reality:** It is easy to build a cool demo (like we saw earlier). It is incredibly hard to move to production.
- **Why?**
    - **Data Quality:** Real-world data is messy, siloed, and unstructured.
    - **Legacy Systems:** AI models don't easily "talk" to 1990s government databases.
    - **Culture:** Fear of replacement leads to resistance.

---

# Slide 29: Critical Challenge 4 - The Adoption Gap (2/2)

**The Adoption Architecture**

- **The "Boring" Rails:** Success depends on the unsexy stuff:
    - **Data Governance:** Treat data as a strategic asset.
    - **Procurement:** Agile contracting instead of 5-year waterfall tenders.
    - **Talent:** Upskilling the broad civil service, not just hiring a few PhDs.
- **Key Takeaway:** You don't need more "AI Strategy" documents. You need "Implementation Playbooks."

---

# Slide 30: The Strategy - A Framework for Action

**Three Pillars for National Success**

1. **Institutional Readiness:** Governance, Data, Talent (The Foundation).
2. **Strategic Use Cases:** Focus on Utility (Reporting, Services), not Magic.
3. **Ecosystem Collaboration:** Research + Government + Private Sector (The Triple Helix).

---

# Slide 31: What Governments Must Do

**Institutional Imperatives**

1. **Fix the Adoption Architecture:** The "boring" rails matter most.
    - Modernize procurement for agile, iterative AI projects
    - Establish clear data governance and sharing frameworks
    - Build internal AI literacy across the civil service
2. **Proactive Labor Market Policy:**
    - Redesign professional development pathways
    - Invest in reinstatement—creating new tasks, not just protecting old jobs
    - Portable benefits and safety nets for workforce transitions
3. **Whole-of-Government Collaboration:**
    - Shared AI infrastructure and platforms
    - Common standards for safety, testing, and accountability
    - Coordinated approach to algorithmic risk

---

# Slide 32: What Organizations Must Do

**Institutional Adaptation**

1. **Redesign Work, Not Just Add AI:**
    - Audit task composition of key roles
    - Identify where AI augments vs. displaces
    - Create new roles for AI oversight, ethics, quality assurance
2. **Protect the Experience Pipeline:**
    - Restructure junior roles to preserve learning
    - Implement "AI-assisted training" that builds judgment
    - Measure development outcomes, not just efficiency
3. **Build Responsible Adoption Culture:**
    - Train all staff, not just specialists
    - Create safe spaces for experimentation
    - Establish clear accountability for AI-assisted decisions

# Slide 33: What Can I Do?

**Personal Agency in the AI Transition**

- **Become AI-Literate:** You don't need to code, but you need to understand capabilities and limitations. Use the tools. Build intuition.
- **Invest in Complementary Skills:** Double down on what AI cannot do—judgment, ethics, stakeholder relationships, context, creativity.
- **Develop Learning Velocity:** The specific tools will change. The ability to rapidly learn and unlearn is the meta-skill.
- **Be a Bridge:** You understand your domain. AI researchers don't. You can translate between technical possibility and operational reality.

---

# Slide 34: What Can I Do? (Continued)

**Practical Steps This Week**

1. **Experiment:** Spend 30 minutes with an AI tool on a real work task. Draft a brief, analyze feedback, stress-test a proposal.
2. **Audit Your Role:** What tasks do you do that are routine and cognitive? What tasks require judgment, context, relationships?
3. **Have the Conversation:** Talk to your team about AI. What are the fears? What are the opportunities? Where are the quick wins?
4. **Champion Pilots:** Identify one low-risk, high-visibility use case in your area. Demonstrate value. Build momentum.
5. **Stay Informed:** Follow developments. The landscape changes monthly. Your expertise will atrophy without continuous updating.
